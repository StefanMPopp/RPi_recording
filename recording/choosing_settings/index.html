    Choosing settings¶

    How to pick resolution, framerate, color mode and format for an experiment.

    Start from the animal, not the camera¶

    Two questions decide almost everything:

    1. How fast does the behavior happen? → sets the framerate
    2. How small is the feature you need to resolve? → sets the resolution

    Everything else follows. Recording at maximum settings "to be safe" produces enormous files, risks dropped frames, and rarely improves the tracking.

    Framerate¶

    Behavior Suggested fps
    Slow movement, position over time 5–15
    General locomotion, trail following 15–30
    Fast movement, interactions 30–60
    Rapid events (strikes, escapes) 60+
    Timelapse, colony-level change 0.1–2

    For most tracking, the framerate needs to be high enough that an individual moves less than about its own body length between frames. Faster than that adds data without adding information.

    Framerate is the cheapest lever on file size — halving it halves the data rate exactly, with no loss of spatial detail.

    Sensor modes and field of view¶

    The IMX477 offers several modes. They differ in two independent ways: the number of pixels, and how much of the sensor is read.

    Mode Max fps Field of view
    4056 × 3040 ~17 Full
    2028 × 1520 ~66 Full (2×2 binned)
    2028 × 1080 ~92 Full width, cropped height
    1332 × 990 ~148 ~66% of the width

    Cropped modes see a smaller area

    1332 × 990 reads only the central part of the sensor. With the arena filling the frame at full field of view, this mode will cut off the edges — not merely show them at lower resolution. Modes with reduced field of view are marked [NN% FoV] in the dropdown.

    For a 30 × 20 cm arena that must stay fully in frame, 2028 × 1520 is usually the right choice. It keeps the full field of view and allows up to 66 fps.

    Recording only part of the frame¶

    When only part of the arena matters — a trail, a bridge, a nest entrance — tick Crop and record just that rectangle. Copying, encoding and writing all scale with the pixels recorded, so a thin strip records far faster and smaller than the full frame.

    Measured on a Pi 4, grayscale, a strip 256 rows tall across the full width:

    Mode Full frame, fastest format Strip, every format
    4056 × 3040 ~4 fps 17.3 fps (sensor max)
    4056 × 2160 ~6 fps 24.3 fps (sensor max)
    2028 × 1520 ~18 fps 62–65 fps

    A crop cannot exceed the sensor mode's maximum fps: the sensor still reads the whole mode, and the crop is taken afterward. For more fps, choose a faster mode and crop that. Above about 60 fps the processor becomes the limit, and color strips are limited by encoding sooner than grayscale ones.

    Crops up to 1920 px wide use the Pi's hardware H.264 encoder even in the 4056-wide modes.

    The crop stays on the same part of the scene when you change sensor mode. A mode that sees less of the sensor (1332 × 990) clips it to what it can see.

    Color mode¶

    Use grayscale unless the analysis needs color. Tracking works on brightness; the color channels add data without adding usable signal.

    Image stacks MJPEG H.264
    Grayscale saving ~3× ~2.5× ~1.5×

    Choose color only for things like distinguishing color-marked individuals or recording color-dependent behavior.

    Format¶

    See file formats for the full comparison. Short version:

    Format Use when
    MJPEG (.avi) Default for tracking. Every frame independent, no motion artifacts
    TIFF stack Highest fidelity needed, low framerate, storage not a concern
    PNG stack Lossless but smaller than TIFF; only below ~15 fps
    H.264 (.mp4) Long recordings where storage dominates — with the caveat below

    H.264 and tracking

    H.264 compresses by describing how frames differ from one another. On fast-moving subjects this produces blocky artifacts exactly where the animal is, which is exactly where the tracker is looking. It is excellent for reviewing footage by eye and risky for automated tracking. If storage forces it, validate the tracking on a short H.264 clip before committing a whole experiment to it.

    Maximum frame rate¶

    Every recording is capped by the slowest of three stages, and which one binds depends on resolution, color mode and format:

    Limit What it is Typical when
    sensor The sensor mode's maximum fps Small frames, or a thin crop
    CPU Capture falls behind while encoding shares the processor High fps with compressed formats
    encoding Compressing each frame MJPEG and compressed stacks at high resolution
    SD card Writing the bytes Uncompressed stacks at high resolution

    Nothing here is estimated. Run Settings → Measure maximum fps once for a resolution and color mode: it records each format flat out for a few seconds (about 25 s in total). The ⓘ next to Format then shows one table — the maximum fps of every format, what limits it, megabytes per frame, and how long a recording fits on the card. The line under the selector summarizes the chosen format and turns red if the selected frame rate is above its maximum.

    Measurements are stored per sensor mode, recorded frame size (see crop) and color mode, so switching to an unmeasured combination shows it as not measured.

    If the frame rate you need is above the maximum¶

    Read the Limited by column — the fix depends on it:

    • sensor — choose a faster sensor mode; nothing else helps. 2028 × 1520 keeps the full field of view at a quarter of the pixels.
    • CPU — record a smaller crop, choose a format that is cheaper to encode (TIFF needs the least CPU), or switch to grayscale.
    • encoding — choose a faster format (the table shows which), switch to grayscale, or lower the resolution.
    • SD card — choose a format with smaller frames, switch to grayscale, or use a faster card. The stack formats are written uncompressed by default for speed; if the card is what limits them, a compressed setting may record faster (see file formats).

    Measure the card once per card

    The SD card limit uses a write speed measured per card (Settings → Recalibrate write speed, run automatically by the first fps measurement). Re-run it after swapping the card. Cards also slow as they fill and age.

    Exposure, gain and aperture¶

    Open Settings → Calibrate camera with the arena, the lighting and the animals in place. The orange frame starts on the whole image; drag its edges onto the arena floor. Everything the window measures is taken from inside that frame.

    Work through it in this order. Each step fixes one variable, and later steps depend on earlier ones.

    1. Lighting. Make the white background as even as possible. Uneven light costs more than any camera setting, because one tracking threshold then cannot fit the whole arena.
    2. Aperture. Run the aperture sweep, described below. It finds the f-stop with the sharpest corners and tells you how much light each f-stop costs.
    3. Speed. Measure how fast the animals move. This gives the longest exposure time that keeps motion blur small.
    4. Exposure time. Click Use this exposure next to the exposure time the speed section suggests.
    5. Gain. Leave it at 1. If the background marker falls below its green range, add light or open the aperture by a stop. Raise the gain only as a last resort, because it amplifies noise along with the image.

    Click Apply, then File → Save profile to keep the result.

    Why fixed exposure¶

    Automatic exposure adjusts brightness all the time. It also aims for mid-gray, so a mostly white arena comes out gray. Tracking tools such as idtracker.ai segment animals with brightness thresholds, which then fit one video but not the next, or not even the whole of one. A fixed exposure time, gain and white balance keep every recording alike.

    The brightness check¶

    Instead of a histogram, the window shows two markers on a black-to-white bar:

    • background: the typical value of the arena floor. Target 200–240: bright, with headroom before white clips.
    • animals: the typical value of the animals. Target 15–90: clearly dark, but not black, so pose estimation keeps detail inside the body.

    Animals are what moves. The window builds an image of the arena from the past few seconds, and only blobs clearly darker than that image count as animals. Dark tape, the arena rim and shadows never move, so they are part of the background image and are not counted. An animal that stays still for several seconds disappears from detection until it moves again.

    Pixels that are pure white are painted red on the preview, and pure black ones blue.

    Speed, exposure time and frame rate¶

    These two settings affect tracking in different ways:

    • Exposure time sets motion blur. Blur merges animals that touch into one blob, fades legs and antennae, and makes the images trackers use to tell individuals apart less distinct.
    • Frame rate sets how far an animal moves between frames. When two animals meet, that distance decides whether the tracker can follow which is which. Identity swaps after encounters are mainly a frame-rate problem.

    Measure live follows the animals for as long as you let it run; a minute or more of normal behavior is a good sample. Analyze a recording… does the same on the first five minutes of an AVI, MP4 or image stack recorded by this app, taking the scale and frame times from its metadata.

    Both report the 95th and 99th percentile and the fastest speed while the animals move. Resting time is left out, so it does not pull the numbers down. The 95th percentile is filled into Plan for, which you can edit. From it the window shows:

    • Exposure time: the longest exposure that keeps the motion blur within Blur allowed, at most one frame. Use this exposure switches to Fixed with that value; the line below compares it with the exposure in use now.
    • how far an animal moves between frames at the current frame rate, in body lengths once you enter the Body length. Up to 0.5 is good, up to 1 is marginal.
    • the frame rate needed to stay within 0.5 body lengths per frame

    Blur allowed is in recorded pixels, 1 px by default. Once the body length and the scale are both entered, it is filled in from the animals' length in pixels: 2 % of it, but at least 1 px and at most 3 px. Larger animals tolerate more blur, but beyond a few pixels blur closes the gap between touching animals and fades thin legs, whatever the body size. Below 1 px it makes no difference, because the lens and the pixel grid already soften edges by about that much. You can still edit the value; changing the body length or scale fills it in again.

    The preview shows a bar as long as the distance an animal covers in one second at the planned speed, with a tick for every frame. Zoom in (Ctrl + scroll) to compare it with the animals.

    Measured speeds understate encounters

    Matches that could be wrong, such as animals touching, are left out of the speed measurement. If animals speed up when they meet, plan for the 99th percentile instead.

    The speed needs the scale (px/cm). The window takes it from the main window; if it is not set there, enter it in the window.

    The aperture sweep¶

    Animals are not needed for this; flat printed targets are better, because they hold still between measurements.

    1. Lay targets with fine black print (text, a printed checkerboard) on the arena floor: one in the center and one in each corner of the orange frame. A printed resolution test chart works too, if it reaches the corners of the frame: only the center and the four corners are measured. A chart is included: lens_calibration.jpg, also at ~/RPi_recording/wiki/docs/recording/lens_calibration.jpg on every Pi.
    2. Open the aperture fully (lowest f-number) and focus on the center target. Focus is most precise when the depth of field is shallowest. Do not refocus after this.
    3. Set the lens ring to an f-stop, choose the same value in the window and click Measure. Exposure is automatic during the measurement, so every f-stop is compared at the same brightness.
    4. Repeat for each f-stop up to f/8.

    For each f-stop, the table shows:

    Column Meaning
    Edge center / Worst corner How many pixels a black-to-white edge is spread over. 1 px is perfectly sharp.
    Evenness The darkest corner's white as a share of the center's (vignetting).
    Light How much more light the f-stop needs than the brightest one measured.

    The recommendation is the f-stop with the most light among those whose corners are within 10 % of the sharpest result and whose evenness is at least 85 %. Wide open, most lenses are soft and darken toward the corners; stopped far down, diffraction blurs them again. Expect the best result two or three stops above the lowest f-number.

    Each full stop halves the light. Make up for it with brighter lighting rather than a longer exposure (more motion blur) or more gain (more noise).

    Worked examples¶

    Slow, needs whole arena, long recordings.

    • 2028 × 1520, full field of view
    • 15 fps
    • Grayscale
    • MJPEG

    Fast, short, small area of interest.

    • 1332 × 990 (cropped field of view is fine — the event is central)
    • 120 fps
    • Grayscale
    • MJPEG

    Very slow, very long, maximum detail.

    • 4056 × 3040
    • 0.5 fps
    • Grayscale
    • PNG stack (slow encoding is irrelevant at this framerate)

    Needs hue, moderate speed.

    • 2028 × 1520
    • 30 fps
    • Color
    • MJPEG — measure first: color roughly triples the data per frame
